## 1. Minkowski's light cone — the primary source

**Source.** Minkowski, H. (1908). *Raum und Zeit.* Lecture delivered before the 80th Assembly of German Natural Scientists and Physicians (Naturforscher-Versammlung), Cologne, 21 September 1908. Published 1909 in *Jahresberichte der Deutschen Mathematiker-Vereinigung* 18:75–88. English translation in Lorentz-Einstein-Minkowski-Weyl, *The Principle of Relativity* (Dover, 1952), pp. 75–91.

The opening line, in the standard English translation:

> The views of space and time which I wish to lay before you have sprung from the soil of experimental physics, and therein lies their strength. They are radical. Henceforth space by itself, and time by itself, are doomed to fade away into mere shadows, and only a kind of union of the two will preserve an independent reality.

Minkowski's central construction: at every event $P$ in four-dimensional spacetime, there are two cones bounded by the trajectory of a hypothetical photon — the *past light cone* (events whose forward light cones contain $P$; the events that could have causally influenced $P$) and the *future light cone* (events $P$'s forward light cone contains; the events $P$ could causally influence). Outside both cones is the *elsewhere* — spacelike separated, causally disconnected.

Three structural features of Minkowski's construction:

1. **The cone is a typed prediction surface.** The future light cone IS the set of all events that current state can causally affect; it is the predictive horizon for the current event. The past light cone IS the set of all events that could have been evidence about current state; it is the evidential horizon.

2. **The cone has a speed-of-light boundary.** $c$ is the substrate-level constraint: no signal propagates faster, so no causal dependency reaches outside the cone. The cone's *opening angle* is what $c$ buys the observer at this event.

3. **The cone's interior is partitioned by proper time.** Inside the future light cone, events are ordered by how much proper time must elapse along some worldline to reach them; the partition is the substrate's prediction-narrowing budget along each worldline.

What this gives us for substrate translation: the light cone is a *structural carrier for the prediction surface at a given event*. The geometry of the cone (its opening angle, its interior partition, its boundary) is the geometry of causal possibility. Minkowski's contribution is that the cone IS the predictive horizon — there is no separate "prediction theory" in relativity; the cone-geometry IS the prediction theory.

## 2. The optimization paradigm — 70 years of CS on axis-1

### 2.1 What the optimization paradigm did

The dominant program of computer science from approximately 1955 to 2025 has been: **given what the code does, make it do that faster.** Concrete instances:

- **Compiler optimization.** Aho-Sethi-Ullman 1986 *Compilers: Principles, Techniques, and Tools* (the Dragon Book). Constant folding, common-subexpression elimination, loop-invariant code motion, register allocation, instruction scheduling. The IR-as-graph view; optimization-as-graph-rewrite. LLVM (Lattner-Adve 2004 *LLVM: A Compilation Framework for Lifelong Program Analysis & Transformation*, CGO) extended this into a unified IR substrate.
- **Microarchitecture.** Tomasulo's algorithm (1967) for out-of-order execution; the Patterson-Hennessy RISC pipeline (1980s); the deep pipeline of the Pentium 4 (2000); the wide superscalar (3+ instructions per cycle by 2005); SIMD/AVX vectorization. Cache hierarchies (Smith 1982 *Cache Memories*, ACM Computing Surveys) — L1, L2, L3, DRAM, the order-of-magnitude latency cliffs that drive most of microarchitectural design.
- **Parallelism.** Amdahl's law (1967) bounding the speedup; Gustafson's law (1988) recovering some headroom; the MPI/OpenMP era; CUDA / GPGPU.
- **Algorithms.** Big-O analysis (Knuth, *The Art of Computer Programming*, 1968 onward). Better algorithms with the same problem statement: $O(n^2) \to O(n \log n) \to O(n)$ within the same task.

What unifies these: each takes the task as fixed, and asks "how do I do this same task with fewer cycles, fewer bytes, fewer joules?" The paradigm's altitude is *axis-1 of recognition #36's variety vector* — computational throughput.

### 2.2 The partial exception that proves the rule: branch prediction

**Source.** Smith, J. E. (1981). *A Study of Branch Prediction Strategies.* In *Proceedings of the 8th Annual Symposium on Computer Architecture* (ISCA '81), Minneapolis, MN, pp. 135–148. ACM/IEEE.

Smith's contribution: a survey and empirical study of branch prediction strategies for high-performance pipelined CPUs. The central observation: in a pipeline, the cost of mispredicting a branch is the entire pipeline depth × instruction width worth of stalled cycles. The cost is large enough that even simple prediction strategies — a 1-bit history, a 2-bit saturating counter — pay massively. Smith's 2-bit counter became the workhorse predictor of the 1980s and 1990s.

**Source.** Yeh, T.-Y. & Patt, Y. N. (1991). *Two-Level Adaptive Training Branch Prediction.* In *Proceedings of the 24th Annual ACM/IEEE International Symposium on Microarchitecture* (MICRO-24), Albuquerque, NM, pp. 51–61.

Yeh-Patt's contribution: a two-level history table — global history register indexes a pattern history table, which holds saturating counters. The result: ~97% accuracy on nine of the ten SPEC benchmarks, vs <93% for single-level schemes. This is the predictor architecture that scaled into the 2000s and 2010s; modern CPUs (TAGE, perceptron predictors) are descendants of Yeh-Patt's two-level structure.

The structural point: **branch prediction is the one place where mainstream CS built genuine prediction infrastructure, and it pays massively.** Modern Intel/AMD CPUs spend a substantial fraction of their die area on the branch predictor; the predictor's accuracy is the gate on how deep the pipeline can be (deeper pipeline → more parallelism per cycle → but also more pipeline-flush cost on misprediction). The branch predictor IS the prediction paradigm operating *inside* the optimization paradigm.

But notice the altitude of the prediction: **the predictor operates at the instruction-stream level.** It predicts which branch direction the next conditional takes; it does not predict *program-state-evolution* at the typed-program level. The prediction surface is one bit per branch, integrated over a history window. This is microscopic prediction; it cannot answer "what set of program states is reachable from the current state in $N$ kintsugi ticks?" because that question requires typed gap-vocabulary the predictor does not have.

### 2.3 The side effects that prove prediction is real

**Source.** Kocher, P. et al. (2018). *Spectre Attacks: Exploiting Speculative Execution.* arXiv:1801.01203; IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy 2019. Plus Meltdown (Lipp et al. 2018, USENIX Security), Foreshadow (Van Bulck et al. 2018), MDS, and the broader speculative-execution side-channel family.

The Spectre/Meltdown family of attacks proved that *speculative execution leaves observable traces in the cache hierarchy*. The CPU's prediction-paradigm machinery (branch predictor + speculative execution + out-of-order completion + cache as side effect) is so deeply integrated into modern microarchitecture that the side effects are unavoidable; they constitute genuine information leaks that the optimization-paradigm framing did not anticipate.

The structural lesson: **prediction is not a free optimization technique applied on top of an otherwise-deterministic execution.** Prediction *changes what work the substrate does* — work is performed speculatively, and the speculative work has side effects that persist even when the prediction was wrong. This is the *cost of the prediction paradigm* operating below the abstraction line. Spectre/Meltdown say that the prediction layer is its own substrate with its own observable behavior — it cannot be hidden under the optimization-paradigm abstraction.

The substrate-pull connection: when mirror lifts prediction to the typed program-state level (the gap-set's resolution-set), the side effects become *first-class typed verdicts* — the `transparency<p>` partial tier, the `imperfect<a, e, l>` carrier, the `failure(reason)` algedonic signal. The substrate makes the cost of prediction visible at the type level rather than letting it persist as a covert cache-residue.
