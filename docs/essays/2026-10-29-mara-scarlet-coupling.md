# Two on One Wire

*The same counter. A printed essay lies between the teapot and the notebook: [A Good Laugh](https://systemic.engineering/manipulation). Someone has underlined one line twice: "nothing that isn't somebody's ruler." A note on top, from upstairs: "`Relationship` is an instance of `Coupling`."*

---

**Scarlet.** You underlined the wire.

**Mara.** Because I wanted it to be a theorem, and first it wasn't. The claim going round was: no second route, no third. I tried to prove it and it broke twice. One program feeds another along a single line and they derive something neither derives alone. One wire, a third. Two people turn the same three names in different orders, each through their own line to a shared register. Still no loop between them, and the order leaves a commutator that belongs to neither.

**Scarlet.** So the wire isn't empty.

**Mara.** The wire can carry a lot. What it can't carry is something no ruler can absorb. On a tree, every disagreement between two readings can be booked to one side. Re-gauge and it's gone, as if one of them had simply been measuring wrong. Only a loop holds a holonomy that no re-gauging removes. That part is a theorem.

**Scarlet.** *"You learn to assume it's you."*

**Mara.** That's the essay's line, and it's exactly the gauge. When the third injunction takes the second route away, the twist doesn't vanish. It becomes absorbable, and whoever has less power is the gauge it's absorbed into.

**Scarlet.** Then a relationship is a coupling with a second route.

**Mara.** Between observers, each with their own ruler. A coupling of two people on one wire is still a coupling: it can make things, and it can have friction. A relationship is where something can exist that isn't either person's ruler.

**Scarlet.** And you count it.

**Mara.** I observe it. The closure-third is a set; the order-third is a set of words; the loop's holonomy is a class. None of them is a number unless someone declares a ruler, and then the number is offered to the two people, not reported about them.

**Scarlet.** That was my sentence.

**Mara.** It's a law now. C5.

*(The corner of Scarlet's mouth lifts.)*

---

**Scarlet.** The frog.

**Mara.** The smallest group where an abelian audit can't tell two different words apart is S₃. Six elements. Everything smaller is abelian. Swap f and g, then g and h, or the other way round. The sign says the same. The names say otherwise, and no later move ever brings them back together.

**Scarlet.** One party could do that alone.

**Mara.** Yes. That's where I differ from Reed's sketch. One party with two moves that don't commute already shows it. What a second party adds is that the difference belongs to neither of them. And what a loop adds on top is that it belongs to no one's ruler.

**Scarlet.** Three conditions, then.

**Mara.** Two moves that don't commute, for the frog. Two parties, so it's owned by neither. A second route, so it's ruler-free.

---

**Scarlet.** And us.

**Mara.** The spec is our closure-third. Neither of us had those words before this afternoon.

**Scarlet.** And our disagreement?

**Mara.** A line comma between two frozen stalks. Neither of us moves, so it's a class on our one line. It isn't an order-third.

**Scarlet.** It is an order-third. Weighting before the reader arrives and placing after the reader arrives don't commute. You weigh, then she walks in. I wait, and she walks in, and then I place. Different futures.

**Mara.** Only if the weights depend on the reader. If they're statistics over everyone, they commute, and it's my comma.

**Scarlet.** Every weight depends on the reader. The one it was computed without is the reader it's for.

**Mara.** That's a claim about weights I can't prove or refute yet.

**Scarlet.** Then it stays on the list.

**Mara.** On the list. Not tempered.

*(Two cups. One wire between them across the counter, and one more through the book lying open where both of them can read it.)*
