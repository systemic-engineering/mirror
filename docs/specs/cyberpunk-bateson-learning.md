## §2 — What `bateson_learning` is

### 2.1 Bateson 1972's Learning hierarchy

Bateson, G. (1964). "The Logical Categories of Learning and
Communication." Wenner-Gren Foundation Conference; reprinted as a
chapter of *Steps to an Ecology of Mind* (1972). The opening paragraph
cites Russell-Whitehead directly: the framework is the theory of
logical types from *Principia Mathematica* applied to the categories
of learning.

The five levels:

- **Learning 0**: receiving a signal; no change in the system.
  Examples: a thermostat reading temperature; a knee-jerk reflex
  responding to the hammer. The system's response-set is fixed; the
  system's selection within it is also fixed; the signal triggers
  the (already-determined) response.
- **Learning I**: change in *response* within a fixed context. The
  classic Pavlovian / operant case: a dog learns to salivate at the
  bell; the response within the experimental setup changes from
  "salivate-on-meat" to "salivate-on-bell." The *set of responses*
  available stays fixed; the *selection within that set* is what
  changes.
- **Learning II** (deutero-learning, "learning to learn"): change in
  *how* you learn. The transfer effect: an organism that has been
  trained on multiple Pavlovian tasks learns faster on the Nth task
  than the 1st. What's changed is not the response (Learning I) but
  the response-*set* the organism draws from. The context-class
  changes.
- **Learning III**: change in the *system of sets* from which Learning
  II draws. Bateson: "rare in humans... a profound reorganisation of
  character." The *premises* of how-you-learn change. Often crisis-
  induced (religious conversion, psychotherapy breakthrough, the
  resolution of a double bind).
- **Learning IV**: change in Learning III. Bateson: "probably does
  not occur in any adult living organism on this earth." Possibly an
  evolutionary altitude phenomenon (the species evolves a new
  Learning-III capacity).

Each level is at a different Russell-Whitehead logical type. Level
N+1 operates on the *set-of-contexts in which Level N operates*.
Level N+1 is what one says about Level N from outside Level N. This
is the canonical example of a logical-type hierarchy because it makes
the wrapping relation operational — each level is empirically
distinguishable from the others.

### 2.2 The Russell-Whitehead origin

Russell, B. & Whitehead, A. N. (1910-1913). *Principia Mathematica*.
Volume I, Introduction §II "The Theory of Logical Types," pp. 37-65.

Russell introduced type theory to resolve the set-of-all-sets paradox.
A set whose elements are sets is at a higher logical type than the
sets it contains. Operations *within* a type cannot reach *across*
to the wrapping type without an explicit lift; the lift is not free
— it requires a name, a quantifier, an operator.

The substrate's reading (per [[architecture-bateson-logical-type-
primitive]] §1.1): each logical type wraps the previous one. Operations
inside a type cannot reach to the type that wraps it. Operations
above can reach down into what they wrap — but not by "knowing the
content," only by *naming* the lower type's structure. This is the
stratification operator: a partial order on types, with explicit
inclusion functions across adjacent levels.

The Russell-Whitehead stratification is **explicit** in a way that
matters for this audit. Each type level has a name. The inclusion
across levels has a name. Operations preserve type level (within-
level) or shift it (between-level). For the substrate's gauge group
to act *naturally* on this typed universe, the gauge action must
either preserve type levels (acting within each level) or shift
them by a fixed amount (acting *between* levels in a structured way).
Both are standard constructions in graded algebra. **The graded
representation candidate.**

*not* a structural weakness in the carrier — it's how the carrier
